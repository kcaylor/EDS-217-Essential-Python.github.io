Part 1: Vital statistics

1. How many rows and how many columns does the dataset have?

✅ Answer

11,171 rows and 21 columns. One row per day. Hold on to the row count, because several of the later questions compare a column’s count against it: 11,171 is what “complete” looks like in this file, and any count below it is a gap somewhere.

✅ Answer

Twenty-one names, and they fall into three groups. Three columns are time labels (Year, Month, Date), two more identify the site (LTER_Site, Station), and the remaining sixteen are nine measurements, six Flag_ columns and Comments. Each flag column sits immediately after the measurement it annotates, which makes the list look at a glance like a neat alternation of measurement, flag, measurement, flag. Nine and six do not pair up, though, and three of the measurements have no flag column at all: Daily_Windspeed_AbsMax_m_s, Moss and Soil20cm.

Two details in the spelling are worth writing down while the list is on screen. First, the flag prefix is Flag_ on four columns and FLAG_ on two, and Python string matching is case sensitive, so any attempt to select “all the flag columns” by name has to account for both spellings. Second, FLAG_Daily_globalrad_total_mjm2 sits directly after a measurement column named Daily_globalrad_total_jcm2, which is megajoules per square metre against joules per square centimetre. No column in this file is called Daily_globalrad_total_mjm2, and the radiation values run into the low thousands, which is an ordinary daily total in J/cm² and an impossible one in MJ/m², so the flag name is probably the one nobody updated. Noticing a mismatch like that from the column names alone is most of what today’s practice is for!

Part 3: What varies, and what doesn’t

Code

✅ Answer

Neither column varies, so neither one can tell you anything about the differences between rows. LTER_Site is ARC in all 11,171 rows and Station is TLKMAIN in all 11,171 rows. A column with one distinct value cannot distinguish one row from another, so it can never explain anything, and grouping by it would return a single group.

They still have a use, though. They record provenance: every row came from the main meteorological station at the Arctic LTER site, so this whole file is one instrument at one place. Provenance is worth a sentence in your biography, because it settles two questions at once. Nothing here needs to be averaged across stations, and these data describe one point on the North Slope rather than the Arctic in general.

9. Use .value_counts() on Flag_Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C. What values appear, and what do you think they mean?

Code 
toolik['Flag_Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C'].value_counts()
Flag_Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C
E    1310
Name: count, dtype: int64
✅ Answer

One value appears, E, on 1,310 days. In the Arctic LTER flag convention, E marks a value that was estimated rather than measured directly, usually filled in from a nearby sensor by regression when the main logger failed. The Comments column says so in words on those rows, with entries such as “Air temp 1 & 5 meter estimated from regression”.

Question 5 found Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C to be the one measurement with no missing values, which made it look like the most trustworthy column in the file. Put the two results together and the picture changes: 1,310 of those 11,171 values, or 11.7%, are flagged as estimated. The column is complete because somebody filled the gaps, not because the instrument never stopped. Counting nulls would never have shown you this, and it is worth knowing before you compute a temperature trend from the column.

Note also that .value_counts() drops nulls by default, which is why the 9,861 unflagged days do not appear. Use .value_counts(dropna=False) when you want to see them.

No, and the two ends of the result show you two different things. .value_counts() sorts by count from largest to smallest, so .head() returns five years with 366 rows each. Seven years in this file have 366 rows, because seven of them are leap years, and every other full year has 365. Five of those seven tie for the top of the list, so which five you see, and in what order, depends on how pandas breaks the tie. Your list may not match the one printed above, which is fine and expected! .tail() returns the other end of the same list, and the short year sits down there: 1988 has 214 rows.

The question asks for both ends because a list sorted by count puts each year wherever its count happens to fall, so a year with fewer observations than its neighbours sorts to the bottom and is invisible from the top. Whenever you use .value_counts() to check whether groups are the same size, read both ends of it.

The 214 days are the start of the record rather than a gap in the middle of a year. Counting back 214 days from 31 December 1988 reaches 1 June, which agrees with the Date minimum of 19880601 you saw in question 4. Toolik began recording in mid-year.

The consequence is a real one. Any per-year summary you compute for 1988 covers June to December only, so it leaves out the coldest five months and will not be comparable with any other year in the file. Whenever a group has fewer rows than its neighbours, find out which rows are missing before you compare the groups.

12. Are the mean, the minimum and the maximum plausible for a site above the Arctic Circle? Say why or why not.

✅ Answer

Mean -8.20 °C, minimum -55.60 °C, maximum 20.70 °C, over all 11,171 days, with a standard deviation of 14.83 °C and a median of -7.10 °C.

All three numbers are plausible for the North Slope of Alaska. A mean well below freezing is what makes this a permafrost site. The extremes are extremes of a daily mean, not of an instantaneous reading, so -55.6 °C is a day that averaged -55.6 °C, which is a good deal more remarkable than a brief cold spike. The quartiles are the useful check on plausibility: 25% of days average below -19.7 °C and 75% are below 4.4 °C, so this is a place that spends most of the year frozen and thaws briefly.

One caution about the mean. It is the average of every day in the file, and the file starts on 1 June 1988, so the first year contributes a summer and no winter. The effect is small here, -8.26 °C if you exclude 1988 entirely against -8.20 °C with it, but the direction is predictable: an unbalanced record pulls the average toward whatever season is over-represented.

✅ Answer

Two Day 2 collections doing two different jobs. The list is an ordered selection: passing it to toolik[...] returns the columns in the order you wrote them, so the list decides both what is kept and how it is arranged. Two things to check in your own version. First, keeping the time columns matters: a table of temperatures with no Year, Month or Date cannot be plotted against time, so the useful five columns are three labels and two measurements. Second, .rename() returns a new DataFrame and does not alter the original, which is why the code reassigns toolik_temps. Confirm this by running toolik.columns.tolist() again: the full table still has Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C under its original name. Keeping units in the new names, as temp_mean_c does, is worth the two extra characters.

Part 6: Write the biography

There is no single right answer here. A strong biography states a number for every claim, names at least one problem that the file does not announce, and gives a fitness-for-purpose verdict with a condition attached rather than an unqualified yes. Here is one that would earn full marks.

✅ A model answer

This file holds daily weather from the main meteorological station (TLKMAIN) at Toolik Field Station, part of the Arctic LTER site (ARC) on the North Slope of Alaska. Both identifier columns hold a single value throughout, so every observation comes from one instrument cluster at one place. The 21 columns cover nine measurements: air temperature (daily mean, absolute maximum, absolute minimum), precipitation, mean and maximum wind speed, global radiation, and moss and 20 cm soil temperature. Six of the nine have a flag column.

Coverage is 11,171 daily rows from 1 June 1988 to 31 December 2018, with every calendar day present. 1988 holds only 214 rows because the record starts in June, so it is a partial year, not a lost one.

Fifteen of the 21 columns have missing values. Daily mean air temperature, complete at 11,171 values, is the column I would trust most. I would not trust global radiation, which has 4,118 values and includes an impossible -2.0 J/cm², or annual precipitation totals, because 420 days are absent and 183 of them fall in 1995 alone.

Two cautions. First, Date is stored as the integer 19880601, not as a date, so it cannot be sorted or differenced as a date until converted. Second, the complete temperature column is not fully measured: 1,310 days, 11.7% of the record, have an E flag meaning the value was estimated from a nearby sensor.

Could this describe change in Arctic summer temperatures since 1988? Yes, with one check first. All 31 years have complete June to August coverage and no missing means, but 85 of the 92 summer days in 1988 are flagged estimated, and no summer day after 2006 has a flag. Because 1988 anchors any trend, I would confirm the result holds with those days excluded.

The optional loop

Where the marks are

If you compare your notebook against this key, look for these four things before you look at anything else.

  1. Every factual claim in your biography has a code cell above it that produced it. A sentence such as “the record covers about thirty years” is the failure mode this exercise is built to catch. “1 June 1988 to 31 December 2018, 11,171 daily rows” is the pass.
  2. You found 1988 and explained it. Reporting that 1988 has 214 rows is half the answer. The mark is for saying why: the record begins on 1 June 1988, so 1988 is a partial year rather than a year with days missing from it, and no per-year figure for 1988 is comparable with any other year in the file.
  3. You read the flag columns as part of the file’s design rather than as errors, and you connected them back to Daily_AirTemp_Mean_C. The column with no missing values contains 1,310 estimated ones, and a biography that reports it as complete without mentioning the flags sends its reader off with the wrong idea of how solid the temperature record is.
  4. Your answers say what a number means rather than what it is. “420 values are missing from precipitation” restates the output. “420 missing days, 183 of them in 1995, so an annual total for that year covers half a year of rain” reads it.
