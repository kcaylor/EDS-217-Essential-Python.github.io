8. In a markdown cell, describe in two or three sentences how the three lists differ from one another. What kind of food rises to the top when the denominator changes from a kilogram to a thousand calories, and what kind rises again when it changes to a hundred grams of protein?

✅ Answer

Ranked per kilogram, the list is meat: four beef products, three lamb, two cheeses and prawns. Ranked per thousand calories, the beef stays but lamb largely drops out and lettuce and raspberries appear, because they are mostly water and you have to eat a great deal of either to obtain a thousand calories. Ranked per hundred grams of protein, three foods with almost no protein in them take the top places: coconut milk, sugar and grapes, because dividing by a number close to zero blows the ratio up. The beef does not leave, though. Beef steak is still fourth, beef mince sixth and beef meatballs tenth, so the protein ranking mixes the genuinely costly foods together with the arithmetic artefacts.

So the answer to the question as asked: moving from a kilogram to a thousand calories promotes bulky, watery foods, because it takes a lot of lettuce or raspberries to reach a thousand calories. Moving again to a hundred grams of protein promotes foods with almost no protein, because the denominator gets very small. Neither move is telling you those foods became worse. Both are telling you the question changed.

10. Questions 8 and 9 asked what the lists say. Now say what it means. In a markdown cell, explain what you learn about a food from the fact that it sits near the top of all three lists at once, and what you learn about a food that tops one list and appears on neither of the others. Name one food of each kind from your answer to question 9, and say which of the two tells you more about the food itself rather than about the denominator.

✅ Answer

A food at the top of all three lists is expensive however you measure it. Its ranking does not depend on the choice of denominator, so no reasonable objection about the basis of comparison rescues it. Beef steak is the clearest case: first by weight, first by calorie, fourth by protein.

A food that tops one list only is telling you about the denominator, not about the food. Sugar is second per hundred grams of protein and appears nowhere else, because sugar has essentially no protein, and a small number divided by a number close to zero is large. The arithmetic behind the ranking is correct, but the claim it seems to make about sugar’s climate cost is misleading.

To answer the last part directly: the food on all three lists tells you more about the food. Beef steak is costly on every basis anyone has proposed, so there is no denominator you could switch to that would rescue it. Sugar’s position tells you about the denominator instead, and it would move the moment you picked a different one.

The practical lesson is that the choice of denominator is an argument, not a technicality. Pick the one that matches what the food is actually for. Compare protein sources per gram of protein and staples per calorie.

12 and 13. The ~ version, the < 1 version, and why they differ.

Code 
no_worse_than_banana = df[~(df['Bananas index (kg)'] > 1)]
print(no_worse_than_banana.shape)

better_than_banana = df[df['Bananas index (kg)'] < 1]
print(better_than_banana.shape)
(26, 11)
(25, 11)
✅ Answer

26 and 25. The difference is exactly one row, and it is Bananas itself.

~(df['Bananas index (kg)'] > 1) keeps every food that is not above 1, which includes foods that equal 1. df['Bananas index (kg)'] < 1 is strictly less than, so it drops them. Only one food in this dataset scores exactly 1.0, and by construction it is the banana.

The general point is that ~(x > 1) means x <= 1, not x < 1. Negating a strict comparison gives you a non-strict one, and the boundary value moves from one side to the other. Whenever a filter returns one row more or fewer than you expected, check the boundary first.

19. Compare your land-use top 10 with your emissions top 10 from question 6. Both are measured per thousand calories, so they are the fair comparison. Are they the same foods? Answer in a markdown cell, and name one food that appears on one list and not the other.

✅ Answer

Seven of the ten foods are the same: beef steak, beef mince, beef meatballs, beef burger, cottage cheese, lamb (leg) and lettuce. So the two measures agree far more than they disagree, and beef sits at the top of both.

Three foods appear only in the land-use list: lentils, lamb chops and chilli con carne. Lentils are the interesting one. Part 7: Write it up

There is no single right answer here. A strong response cites at least three numbers you computed, names at least one disagreement between the rankings, and reaches a conclusion the numbers support. Here is one that would earn full marks.

✅ A model answer

Cutting bananas is not where the gains are. A banana scores 1.0 on the kilogram index by definition, and it sits at 135th of 160 foods on that ranking. Beef steak scores 148.6 on the same scale, so a kilogram of steak emits roughly 149 times what a kilogram of bananas emits, and per thousand calories beef steak needs 82.3 times the land a banana needs. Removing bananas from a diet changes a very small number and creates a gap that has to be filled by something else.

The dataset does make bananas look worse under one measure. Per hundred grams of protein they rise to 63rd of 160, above eggs, chicken breast, salmon, tuna, cod and pork chops, because a banana has almost no protein and the division inflates the ratio. The disagreement between the rankings is the point rather than a flaw. Coconut milk, sugar and grapes top the protein ranking for the same arithmetic reason, and nobody would argue that sugar is a worse climate choice than beef. The comparison is only meaningful when the denominator matches what the food is for, and bananas are not a protein source.

If my friend wants a change that shows up in the numbers, the place to look is the 34 foods scoring above 10 per kilogram, which is a little over a fifth of this dataset and is dominated by beef, lamb and cheese. Substituting one of those a week would move more carbon than eliminating bananas entirely.

  1. Every number in your Part 7 write-up has a code cell above it that produced it. Quoting a number you did not compute is the single most common way to lose marks on this exercise.
  2. Your Part 4 boundary answer is right. Questions 12 and 13 differ by one row, and knowing which row and why is the point of asking both.
  3. You looked at the cheese labels rather than trusting the count.
  4. Your interpretation answers say what the number means, not what the number is. “34 foods score above 10” restates the output. “34 foods, a fifth of the dataset, and almost all of them are meat or cheese” reads it.
